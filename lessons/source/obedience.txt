== opening | 4
Ask parents in the class: "Have your children ever 'mostly' obeyed you? Cleaned most of their room, or done the homework except for the hard part?" Ask: "Does 'mostly' count as obeying?" Today we see a king who mostly obeyed God, and what God thought of it.

== scripture | 3
Read the passage aloud, with readers for Samuel and Saul if possible.

== background | 5
Saul was Israel's first king. He began humbly. Samuel reminds him that he was "little in thine own sight" (v. 17). Earlier he had already disobeyed by offering a sacrifice he was told to wait for (13:8-14). In chapter 15 God, through Samuel, commanded Saul to bring judgment on the Amalekites, the people who had attacked Israel in the wilderness (Exodus 17:8-16; Deuteronomy 25:17-19), and to take no plunder.

Saul won the battle but kept King Agag alive and kept the best of the livestock. Then he built a monument to himself (v. 12). When Samuel arrived, Saul greeted him by announcing, "I have performed the commandment of the LORD."
note: The command to destroy the Amalekites troubles many readers today. It was a specific act of judgment on a particular nation at a particular time and is not a pattern for God's people now. The lesson's focus, like the passage's, is on Saul's response to God's word.

== point | 7 | Partial obedience is disobedience
Saul claimed full obedience while the bleating of the sheep gave him away (v. 14). He had done the parts of the command that suited him and skipped the rest. God did not grade him on a curve. Choosing which parts of God's word to obey means we, not God, are really in charge.
verses: 1 Samuel 15:13-15; James 2:10; Luke 6:46

== point | 7 | Excuses and blame
Twice Saul blamed "the people" (vv. 15, 21), and he gave a religious excuse: the animals were for sacrifice. Matthew Henry notes how quickly those who justify themselves blame others. Real repentance says "I have sinned" without "but."

== point | 7 | God wants obedience more than sacrifice
"Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams" (v. 22). Sacrifices were commanded by God. But religious activity cannot make up for a disobedient heart. Samuel compares stubborn rebellion to witchcraft and idolatry, because it puts our will in God's place. For Christians, obedience flows from love: "If ye love me, keep my commandments" (John 14:15).
verses: 1 Samuel 15:22-23; Micah 6:6-8; John 14:15

== questions | 7
q: Why do you think Saul kept the best animals and King Agag? | Pride, greed, the people's pressure, wanting a trophy. He valued what looked good to him above God's word.
q: What "religious" excuses do we use to cover disobedience? | Doing good things to avoid the thing God asked; "God understands"; busyness in church instead of obedience at home.
q: Why is blaming others so natural, and what does it cost us? | It protects our pride; it keeps us from repentance and damages relationships.
q: How is obedience out of love different from obedience out of fear or duty? | Love obeys gladly and wholeheartedly; fear or bare duty tends toward the minimum and toward excuses.

== application | 3
Ask God to show you one area where you have been "mostly" obeying him. Name it honestly, without excuses, and take one concrete step of full obedience this week.

== prayer | 2
Lord God, forgive us for the times we have done what suited us and called it obedience. Forgive our excuses and our blaming. Give us hearts that love you and hearken to your voice, for to obey is better than sacrifice. Help us follow you fully, in the strength of Jesus, who was obedient to the Father in all things. Amen.
